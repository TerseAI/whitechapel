# Interview writing

Whitechapel interviews connect a witness’s motives, the player’s questions and the evidence already found. For historical testimony and public discussion of the murders, see [the reading list](reading-list.md).

## How the dialogue should sound

### Give each person a reason to be in the conversation

Before drafting a topic, write down what the investigator needs and what the witness wants right now. Keep those notes outside the spoken dialogue.

Use contractions where the speaker would. Let an answer be a few words when that is all the question needs. Avoid phonetic accent spelling and decorative Victorian language. An occasional longer recollection earns its space by revealing something the short answers could not.

As a drafting target, most turns should fit in one or two spoken sentences. Split a long answer where the investigator would naturally interrupt or ask the next question. Leave room for a precise follow-up before moving to another subject.

### Make the next line depend on the last

A useful sequence is:

1. Ask about one person, object, place or action.
2. Let the witness answer, qualify, refuse or evade that particular question.
3. Follow the new detail or the evasion.
4. End with something changed: a location to check, a corrected account, an admission, a refusal, or a remaining gap the player understands.

Read the exchange without topic labels, approach labels, rewards or explanatory UI. It must still make sense. A sudden new subject needs a spoken bridge. A greeting should establish why the detectives are there without assuming they visited another location first.

### Make the choices understandable before they are spoken

The player should see the investigator's intended words. Reassuring a frightened person, pressing for an omitted detail and presenting a contradiction are different actions; none should be a disguised instruction to select the “correct” emotion.

Write plausible alternatives. Do not make every wrong choice an absurd demand for certainty or an immediate murder accusation. An unsuccessful approach can produce a brief answer, protect a secret, damage cooperation or leave a question unresolved. The response must make that consequence understandable.

For an evidence challenge, write the whole chain: **the witness's claim → the selected object's actual content → the investigator's objection → the witness's answer**. A valid contradiction should make the witness address it. An unrelated object should receive an answer about its irrelevance. Do not use one speech that magically describes every possible selection.

Ordinary follow-up questions can be automatic dialogue beats. Reserve player choices for decisions that change the approach or what gets established. If a topic closes after one attempt, give its outcome a clear reason and preserve another route to any essential finding.

## Notes and evidence should belong to the same world

Give each document an author or custodian, a practical purpose, and contents that fulfil that purpose. A clerk records a payment. A constable records a patrol. A frightened letter-writer tries to persuade somebody. Their language should reflect that job.

| Material | What belongs on it |
| --- | --- |
| Ledger | Names, entries, amounts, dates when supported, corrections and a credible reason for the book to exist. |
| Witness statement | What this person said they saw or heard, where they were, and any uncertainty they expressed. |
| Investigator's note | Concise attributed facts and a clearly marked unanswered question. Preserve disagreement between sources. |
| Letter | The message the writer wants its recipient to read, including evasions or threats appropriate to that writer. |
| Newspaper | A report with its own source and level of certainty. An allegation remains attributed to whoever made it. |
| Object or plan | Observable marks, geometry, wear, writing or measurements. Include enough context to understand what is being examined. |

Never put an unspoken admission into a saved statement. A brief or unsuccessful interview must produce notes appropriate to that outcome. A quote must match the spoken words; an investigator's summary should be presented as a summary.

Check each fact’s date, source and scope. An alibi for one night does not answer an allegation about another. Matching initials alone do not establish that two records name the same person. Review spoken claims, evidence and resulting deductions together.

Keep historical attribution in the research dossier and source information. Authored game exhibits can be convincing while remaining clearly distinguished from surviving historical documents. Suspense comes from a detail the player can examine and a question it leaves open.

## Review before recording

For every topic, place the opening question, initial answer, each selectable line, its actual response and its resulting note together in one review sheet. Include missing evidence, wrong evidence, changed topic order and returning to a saved interview. Check the sequence as a conversation, not as isolated strings.

- Can a listener explain what the investigator asked and how the witness answered?
- Does each person have a recognisable concern and voice?
- Is every named object already introduced or visibly being presented?
- Does each branch react to the words and evidence the player chose?
- Does a useful answer reveal something specific? Does an unsuccessful one fail for an understandable reason?
- Can the notes and source pages be traced back to the facts actually established?
- Does the player still have a discovery or inference to make?
- Can the exchange be shortened without losing context, character or a necessary fact?

Read both parts aloud before synthesising them. Word counts, lint and audio-file checks can expose problems, but they cannot establish that a scene works. Finish the writing pass first; then generate and listen to the revised exchanges with their subtitles and Case Map entries.
